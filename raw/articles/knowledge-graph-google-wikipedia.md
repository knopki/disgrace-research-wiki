Knowledge panel data about Thomas Jefferson displayed on Google Search, as of January 2015

The **Knowledge Graph** is a [knowledge base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_base "Knowledge base") from which [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google "Google") serves relevant information in an infobox beside its [search results](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_engine_results_page "Search engine results page"). This allows the user to see the answer in a glance, as an [instant answer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_answer "Instant answer"). The data is generated automatically from a variety of sources, covering places, people, businesses, and more.[^20] [^21]

The information covered by Google's Knowledge Graph grew quickly after launch, tripling its data size within seven months (covering 570 million entities and 18 billion facts [^1]). By mid-2016, Google reported that it held 70 billion facts [^2] and answered "roughly one-third" of the 100 billion monthly searches they handled. By May 2020, this had grown to 500 billion facts on 5 billion entities.[^22]

There is no official documentation of how the Google Knowledge Graph is implemented.[^3] According to Google, its information is retrieved from many sources, including the *[CIA World Factbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Factbook "The World Factbook")* (discontinued in February 2026) and [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia "Wikipedia").[^23] It is used to answer direct spoken questions in [Google Assistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Assistant "Google Assistant") [^4] [^5] and [Google Home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Home_\(platform\) "Google Home (platform)") voice queries.[^6] It has been criticized for providing answers with neither source attribution nor [citations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citation "Citation").[^7]

## History

Google announced its Knowledge Graph on May 16, 2012, as a way to significantly enhance the value of information returned by Google searches.[^23] Initially available only in English, it was expanded in December 2012 to [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language "Spanish language"), [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language"), [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language"), [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language "Portuguese language"), [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language "Japanese language"), [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language") and [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language "Italian language").[^8] [Bengali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_language "Bengali language") support was added in March 2017.[^24]

The Knowledge Graph was powered in part by [Freebase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freebase_\(database\) "Freebase (database)").[^23]

In August 2014, *[New Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scientist "New Scientist")* reported that Google had launched a **Knowledge Vault** project.[^9] After publication, Google reached out to *[Search Engine Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_Engine_Land "Search Engine Land")* to explain that Knowledge Vault was a research report, not an active Google service. *Search Engine Land* expressed indications that Google was experimenting with "numerous models" for gathering meaning from text.[^10]

Google's Knowledge Vault was meant to deal with facts, automatically gathering and merging information from across the Internet into a knowledge base capable of answering direct questions, such as "Where was [Madonna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madonna_\(entertainer\) "Madonna (entertainer)") born?" In a 2014 report, the Vault was reported to have collected over 1.6 billion facts, 271 million of which were considered "confident facts" deemed to be more than 90% true. It was reported to be different from the Knowledge Graph in that it gathered information automatically instead of relying on crowd-sourced facts compiled by humans.[^10]

## Features

### Google Knowledge Panel

A Google Knowledge Panel [^11] which is part of Google search engine result pages, presents an overview of entities such as individuals, organizations, locations, or objects directly within the search interface. This feature uses data from Google Knowledge Graph,[^12] an extensive database that organizes and interconnects information about entities, enhancing the retrieval and presentation of relevant content to users.

## Criticism

### Lack of source attribution

By May 2016, knowledge boxes were appearing for "roughly one-third" of the 100 billion monthly searches the company processed.[^7] Dario Taraborelli, head of research at the [Wikimedia Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikimedia_Foundation "Wikimedia Foundation"), told *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post "The Washington Post")* that Google's omission of sources in its knowledge boxes "undermines people’s ability to verify information and, ultimately, to develop well-informed opinions". The publication also reported that the boxes are "frequently unattributed", such as a knowledge box on the age of actress [Betty White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_White "Betty White"), which is "as unsourced and absolute as if handed down by God".[^7]

### Declining Wikipedia article readership

According to *[The Register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Register "The Register")* in 2014, the display of direct answers in knowledge panels alongside Google search results caused significant readership declines for [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia "Wikipedia"), from which the panels obtained some of their information.[^13] Also in 2014, *[The Daily Dot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Dot "The Daily Dot")* noted that "Wikipedia still has no real competitor as far as actual content is concerned. All that's up for grabs are traffic stats. And as a nonprofit, traffic numbers don't equate into revenue in the same way they do for a commercial media site". After the article's publication, a spokesperson for the [Wikimedia Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikimedia_Foundation "Wikimedia Foundation"), which operates Wikipedia, stated that it "welcomes" the knowledge panel functionality, that it was "looking into" the traffic drops, and that "We've also not noticed a significant drop in search engine referrals. We also have a continuing dialog with staff from Google working on the Knowledge Panel".[^14]

In his 2020 book, [Dariusz Jemielniak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dariusz_Jemielniak "Dariusz Jemielniak") noted that as most Google users do not realize that many answers to their questions that appear in the Knowledge Graph come from Wikipedia, this reduces Wikipedia's popularity, and in turn limited the site's ability to raise new funds and attract new volunteers.[^15]

### Bias

The algorithm has been criticized for presenting biased or inaccurate information, usually because of sourcing information from websites with high [search engine optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_engine_optimization "Search engine optimization"). It had been noted in 2014 that while there was a Knowledge Graph for most major historical or pseudo-historical [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion") figures such as [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses "Moses"), [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad") and [Gautama Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha "Gautama Buddha"), there was none for [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus "Jesus"), the central figure of [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity").[^16] [^17] On June 3, 2021, a knowledge box identified [Kannada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada "Kannada") as the ugliest language in India, prompting outrage from the Kannada-language community; the state of [Karnataka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karnataka "Karnataka"), where most Kannada speakers live, also threatened to sue Google for damaging the public image of the language. Google promptly changed the featured snippet for the search query and issued a formal apology.[^18] [^19]
